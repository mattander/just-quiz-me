# Just Quiz Me Quiz Craft

## Product Promise

Just Quiz Me is a fast, weird, highly shareable internet quiz site. People land on a quiz, answer a short set of multiple-choice questions, get a ridiculous result, and immediately want to send it to a friend.

The concept is part BuzzFeed throwback, part joke machine. The site should feel clean, refined, and focused; the content carries the personality.

## The Missing Quality

The magic ingredient is sharable self-recognition with a little sting.

The writing should not merely describe personality archetypes. It should catch the player in a tiny social truth:

- "That is me."
- "That is my friend."
- "I hate how accurate this is."
- "I need someone else to take this because I already know what they are."

This is horoscope logic. It does not need to be scientifically true. It needs to be emotionally available, flattering enough to accept, and specific enough to feel personal.

## House Voice

Use this voice:

- Calm, clean, observant, and sly.
- Affectionate roast, not insult comedy.
- Specific social observation, not random absurdity.
- Modern but not trend-chasing.
- Weird in the premise, grounded in the behavior.

Avoid this voice:

- Overly zany.
- Random object soup.
- Meme slang pileups.
- Corporate quiz marketing.
- Clinical self-help.
- "AI trying to be quirky."

Good tonal center:

> You are safe to be quiet around, but absolutely not safe to lend a hoodie to near a body of water.

Bad tonal center:

> You are feral chaos goblin energy with maximum cursed snack vibes.

## Quiz Architecture

Use six questions by default. Six is long enough for pattern recognition and anticipation, but still short enough for a casual shareable quiz.

Recommended arc:

1. **Recognition:** an easy behavior the player can answer without thinking too hard.
2. **Social context:** what the player does around friends, coworkers, strangers, or a group chat.
3. **Pressure:** what happens when stakes rise, time runs out, or someone behaves badly.
4. **Object or scene:** a concrete prop, place, tab, cart, hoodie, mug, sign-off, or other vivid anchor.
5. **Gently incriminating flaw:** the answer that exposes how the player rationalizes themselves.
6. **Final verdict:** an image or decision that feels like the quiz has enough evidence now.

For each question, include three answer options by default. Each answer should correspond primarily to one result. Mixed weights are useful for nuance, but the player experience should still feel clear.

## Titles

Titles are the hook and should work out of context in a feed, message, or group chat.

Strong title formula:

- "Which [specific absurd category] Are You?"
- "Which [ordinary object] Would [specific socially loaded action]?"
- "What Kind of [social chaos / folklore / tiny disaster] Are You?"

Good:

- Which Greek God Would Block You on Slack?
- Which Kitchen Appliance Would Betray You in the Civil War?
- Which Parking Lot Bird Is Judging You?

Needs more work:

- What Is Your Personality Type?
- Which Chaotic Thing Are You?
- What Does Your Breakfast Say About You?

The best titles combine a familiar identity format with one sharp mismatch. The mismatch should imply a world, not just randomness.

## Questions

Questions should create a tiny stage where the player reveals themselves.

Prefer:

- A specific situation.
- A choice under mild social pressure.
- A behavior a friend could recognize.
- A scene that belongs to the quiz premise.

Avoid:

- Abstract trait questions.
- Questions whose answers are only synonyms for the results.
- Long setup paragraphs.
- "Pick a vibe" unless the choices are concrete and funny.

Good question patterns:

- "A coworker books a Friday 4:30 p.m. meeting. What is your move?"
- "A snack is visible in your hand. What is your defensive posture?"
- "The hoodie comes back. What condition tells you everything?"
- "Someone says, 'Can we just make this quick?' What happens inside you?"

Weaker pattern:

- "What is your communication style?"
- "How chaotic are you?"
- "Pick an energy."

## Answer Options

Answer options are where the quiz earns trust. They must make sense.

Rules:

- Make answers behavior-first.
- Make them mutually distinct.
- Keep them scannable.
- Give each answer a tiny emotional motive.
- Let answers be funny because they are recognizable.
- Include at least one answer per quiz that feels gently incriminating.

Better:

- "Accept it, say very little, and remember who did this."
- "A very good pen that you do not lend out because trust has limits."
- "One perfectly timed screenshot that changes the case."

Weaker:

- "Be chaotic and mysterious."
- "Choose the cursed option."
- "You are reliable, but nobody has technically seen you since Tuesday."

The player should never wonder, "What does this even mean?" They can wonder, "Why does this know me?"

## Results

Results are the share object. Write them as identity badges, not reports.

Each result needs:

- **Recognition:** what kind of person this result catches.
- **Affectionate sting:** the funny flaw or tell.
- **Premise evidence:** how the quiz world interprets the player.
- **Share line:** a compact sentence that would work in a screenshot, social post, or text.

Useful result structure:

1. "You are..." line with an immediate recognizable truth.
2. Specific detail from the quiz world.
3. Comic consequence.
4. Final shareable line.

Example shape:

> You are the person who turns panic into a plan, then asks one more clarifying question after everyone else has spiritually left the room. Athena respects the structure, but blocks you when the thread becomes a second workplace. Your gift is making chaos legible; your curse is naming every subtask out loud.

Every result should feel like a plausible "omg so true" even if the player wanted a different one.

## Horoscope Effect

The quiz is not diagnosing people. It is giving them a playful interpretive lens.

To create the horoscope effect:

- Make each result partly flattering.
- Include a vulnerability people can accept.
- Use traits many people can recognize, but attach them to specific evidence.
- Avoid making one result obviously "best."
- Avoid making one result obviously "bad."
- Make the result feel socially useful: a friend should understand why this was sent to them.

Good result tension:

- competent but overprepared
- warm but evasive
- funny but deflecting
- calm but keeping receipts
- generous but bad at boundaries
- organized but emotionally expensive

## Humor Mechanics

Use comedy as precision, not noise.

Reliable tools:

- **Specificity:** "Friday 4:30 p.m. meeting" beats "bad meeting."
- **Contrast:** a grand premise applied to a tiny modern behavior.
- **Status shift:** an ordinary object becomes judge, traitor, ghost, or oracle.
- **Recognition:** the player sees a real habit from an outside angle.
- **Escalation:** each question should feel a little more committed to the bit.
- **Understatement:** let absurd premises be described with calm seriousness.

Avoid:

- Randomness without social logic.
- Too many punchlines in a row.
- Explaining why the premise is funny.
- Repeating the same sentence rhythm across all options.

## Consistency Checks

Run this checklist before shipping or committing quiz copy:

- Does the title make someone smile before they click?
- Can each answer be picked by a real person?
- Does every answer imply a different result without feeling mechanical?
- Do the questions escalate across the six-question arc?
- Is there one answer a player might send to a friend with "this is you"?
- Does every result feel shareable?
- Would every result make someone feel seen, not merely categorized?
- Is the copy calm enough for the joke to breathe?
- Did we remove any forced slang, generic "chaos" words, or random weirdness?
- Did we avoid punching down?

## Revision Moves

When copy feels flat:

- Replace a trait with a behavior.
- Replace a generic setting with a specific scene.
- Add a social witness.
- Add a tiny cost or consequence.
- Make the final result line more quotable.
- Cut one clever phrase so the best one lands.

When copy feels too zany:

- Ask what real human behavior the line is about.
- Remove any image that does not serve that behavior.
- Use one specific concrete object instead of three random ones.
- Make the answer make sense before making it funny.

When copy feels too tame:

- Add a sharper social situation.
- Add a flaw the player can admit.
- Make one option more incriminating.
- Make the result consequence more theatrical while keeping the psychology real.
